Project Liquid · Propulsion

Spark Igniter

A compact augmented spark igniter for a student-built liquid rocket engine. My work focused on the parts of the subsystem I directly owned: mechanical packaging, CAD, manufacturability, hardware integration, and the test path needed to bring the igniter from concept to engine-ready hardware.

My Scope

Rather than presenting this as a full team-system overview, this page focuses on the hardware areas I owned. I was responsible for turning the ignition concept into a compact mechanical assembly that could be machined, sealed, mounted, inspected, and connected to the rest of the propulsion system.

That meant working through the small details that decide whether propulsion hardware is useful in practice: electrode placement, access for assembly, heat exposure, sealing surfaces, fittings, mounting features, and compatibility with the larger test workflow.

Design Areas I Owned

My design work centered on the physical igniter package. I worked through how the spark source, local flow path, sealing interfaces, and mounting geometry fit into one compact assembly without making the part hard to machine or service.

The core challenge was packaging. The igniter needed to place the electrode where it could create a useful ignition source, keep the high-voltage path isolated from nearby grounded hardware, and still leave enough room for fittings, tools, and installation into the engine.

Manufacturing + Assembly

I kept the part geometry grounded in what we could actually make and inspect. The design avoided unnecessary part count, used simple machining operations where possible, and kept critical interfaces visible enough to check before testing.

I also focused on assembly order. Small propulsion parts can become difficult to use if fittings, wires, or seals block each other, so the layout was built around service access, clean routing, and repeatable setup between tests.

Testing Areas I Supported

My testing role was tied to the hardware I owned: making sure the igniter could be installed, connected, checked, and fired in a controlled way before engine-level use. The test path was built to reveal practical problems early, especially issues with fit, sealing, spark placement, routing, and repeatability.

Packaging

Integration

Designed around the larger engine architecture so the igniter could fit into real test operations.

Verification

Helped define what needed to be checked before trusting the igniter in the full system.

Outcome

The result of my work was a more mature igniter package: a design that moved beyond a simple spark concept and addressed the mechanical details needed for installation, testing, and integration with the larger engine.

The most valuable outcome was learning how much of propulsion design happens at the interfaces. A small subsystem still has to satisfy thermal, electrical, fluid, manufacturing, and operations constraints at the same time.

Reflection

This project taught me that small hardware can carry a large amount of system risk. The igniter was physically small, but it forced decisions across mechanical design, electrical safety, fluids, manufacturing, and test operations. It also gave me a stronger appreciation for owning interfaces: the places where a design stops being a CAD model and has to work with tools, people, procedures, and the rest of the engine.
